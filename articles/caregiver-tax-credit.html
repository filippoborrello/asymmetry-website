New Caregiver Tax Legislation Skips the Families Who Need It the Most

Because the Credit for Caring Act is nonrefundable, the caregivers who give up the most income to provide care would receive the least help.

By Chenxi Xu · 4 min read

In high school, while building a social app for seniors, I visited assisted living facilities around Great Falls, Virginia. I expected to meet lonely people, but not so many family members who had scaled back their own lives to care for them. Take Silvia, whose father lived in one. She dropped to part-time hours so she could drive him to his appointments, and still came most evenings to check that he had eaten. Similar to Silvia, about 59 million Americans provide unpaid care to an adult relative or friend.1 For many, caregiving is a second job that pays nothing and costs an average of $7,242 a year, roughly 26 percent on average of caregivers’ income.2 In March 2025, bipartisan lawmakers reintroduced the Credit for Caring Act, which would give working family caregivers a nonrefundable tax credit worth 30 percent of their care expenses above $2,000, up to $5,000 a year.3

The idea sounds nice, but its design is fundamentally flawed. Because the credit is nonrefundable, it lowers what a taxpayer owes but never puts money back in their pocket. A caregiver who owes $1,200 and qualifies for the full $5,000 receives only $1,200, while someone who owes nothing receives nothing. The most strained caregivers have often reduced their hours or left work entirely. Since lower earnings lead to a smaller tax bill, and a smaller tax bill leads to a smaller credit, the policy helps caregivers less the more they have sacrificed. Worse still, the bill requires at least $7,500 in earned income, which shuts out anyone who quit their job to care for a parent full-time.4

To fix this, Congress should make the credit refundable, meaning it pays its full value even when that exceeds what a person owes. The caregiver who owes $1,200 would have her tax bill erased and receive the other $3,800 as a check, and someone who owes nothing would get the full $5,000. Working families already get this through the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), and a Tax Policy Center study found that extending the EITC to low-income caregivers would send almost four-fifths of the benefits to families in the bottom 40 percent of the income distribution.5

Refundability is not free. A refundable credit does not just cancel taxes but sends cash to people who owe none, so its cost climbs with every low-income caregiver who claims the full amount. Cash refunds also reward overstating expenses that the IRS struggles to verify. The IRS estimated that about a third of EITC payments in fiscal 2025, or $21.1 billion, were improper.6 Measures aimed at limiting malicious claims include requiring a doctor’s certification of a medical expense, but additional measures could be enacted to limit unexpected costs of refundability.

As the population ages, unpaid family care is becoming one of the largest hidden costs in the American economy. If Congress wants to recognize that work, it should start with the people doing the most of it for the least.
